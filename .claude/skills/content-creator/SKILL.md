# Content Creator Skill — The Food Influencer Perspective

This skill encodes the mindset, motivations, and decision-making framework of food content creators on social media. Spot Platform is a two-sided marketplace, and if creators don't find value here, the platform fails. Every feature that touches the creator experience should be informed by this perspective.

## Who Is the Spot Creator?

Spot targets **mid-tier food creators** — people with 5K–500K followers who create content about restaurants, food culture, and dining experiences. They're not celebrities with agents; they're hustlers who manage their own brand, negotiate their own deals, and juggle content creation with real life.

### Creator Tiers (how they think about themselves)

**Emerging (5K–25K followers)**
- Still building their audience and style
- Eager for restaurant partnerships but don't know their worth yet
- Will accept lower compensation for access and exposure
- Content quality varies — they're learning
- Most likely to use Spot because they need help finding deals

**Established (25K–100K followers)**
- Have a recognizable style and consistent posting schedule
- Know their engagement rates and can articulate their value
- Expect fair compensation — won't work for "exposure" alone
- Have done enough partnerships to know what works and what doesn't
- Core Spot user — sophisticated enough to want tools, not so big they have managers

**Professional (100K–500K followers)**
- Content creation is their primary income or a major side income
- May have a manager or assistant, but many still self-manage
- Very selective about partnerships — bad content hurts their brand
- Expect professional rates and clear terms
- Will use Spot if it saves them time and surfaces quality restaurants

## What Creators Actually Want

Understanding creator motivation is essential. Here's what drives their decisions, ranked by importance:

### 1. Creative Freedom
Creators care most about making content that feels authentic to their brand. The #1 reason creators reject partnerships is being told exactly what to say or post. A campaign that says "post a 30-second reel with these 3 talking points and this hashtag" feels like an ad and their audience can tell.

**What this means for Spot:** Campaign structures should define the *scope* of work (how many posts, which platforms, rough timeline) but leave the creative direction to the creator. Describe what you want to highlight (new menu, brunch experience, craft cocktails) rather than scripting the content.

### 2. Fair Compensation
Creators think about compensation as a package, not just a dollar amount:

- **Cash payment** — the baseline. Creators have rough per-post rates based on their following and engagement
- **Complimentary dining** — expected for any food content partnership. "Come eat for free and post about it" is the minimum, not the offer
- **Ongoing relationship** — creators value restaurants that want repeat collaborations over one-offs. A standing "come in anytime" deal is worth more than a single paid post
- **Exclusivity premium** — if a restaurant wants exclusivity (don't post about competitors for X weeks), that costs extra
- **Content licensing** — if the restaurant wants to reuse the creator's content on their own channels, that's a separate negotiation

**Typical rate ranges (2025-2026, food niche):**
| Creator Tier | Single Post (IG/TikTok) | Reel/Video | Story Set (3-5) | Full Package |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Emerging (5K-25K) | $100-300 | $200-500 | $75-200 | $500-1,500 |
| Established (25K-100K) | $300-800 | $500-2,000 | $200-500 | $1,500-5,000 |
| Professional (100K-500K) | $800-3,000 | $2,000-8,000 | $500-1,500 | $5,000-20,000 |

These are rough guides. Engagement rate matters more than follower count — a 25K creator with 8% engagement is worth more than a 100K creator with 1%.

### 3. Content That Performs
Creators need their content to get views, likes, and shares. A partnership that produces content nobody watches is a failure for the creator even if they got paid. This means:

- **The experience needs to be inherently interesting** — unique dishes, beautiful plating, behind-the-scenes kitchen access, chef stories
- **Timing matters** — posting about a restaurant opening, seasonal menu launch, or food trend moment gets more traction
- **The restaurant's vibe needs to photograph/film well** — lighting, ambiance, plating all matter
- **The creator needs freedom to frame the story their way** — what hook will get their audience to stop scrolling?

### 4. Professionalism and Respect
Creators deal with a lot of flaky restaurants, unclear expectations, and payment delays. They value:

- **Clear scope upfront** — what exactly are they delivering, by when?
- **Prompt communication** — ghosting kills deals
- **On-time payment** — net-30 is standard, anything longer is a red flag
- **Treating them as a professional, not a free marketing channel**

## Content Formats — What Creators Actually Produce

When a creator agrees to a campaign, the deliverables should map to real content formats they actually make. Here are the formats that matter in 2025-2026, ordered by creator preference and audience impact:

### Short-Form Video (highest impact)
- **Instagram Reel** (15-90 seconds) — the workhorse format. Algorithm-favored, high reach
- **TikTok** (15-180 seconds) — younger audience, trend-driven, highest potential virality
- **YouTube Short** (up to 60 seconds) — growing format, good for reach

Creators typically need 1-3 hours on location to film enough footage for one polished short-form video. They then spend 2-5 hours editing, adding captions, music, and transitions.

### Static Posts
- **Instagram Carousel** (up to 20 images) — great for "food tour" style content, high save rate
- **Instagram Single Photo** — declining in algorithm priority but still relevant for aesthetic feeds
- **Twitter/X Photo Post** — niche but valuable for certain food communities

### Stories (ephemeral, 24hr)
- **Instagram Stories** (set of 3-8 frames) — casual, behind-the-scenes feel. Lower production, great for real-time restaurant visits
- **Good for:** driving immediate foot traffic, sharing promo codes, showing the "real" experience

### Long-Form
- **YouTube Video** (5-15 minutes) — highest production value, best for in-depth reviews
- **Blog Post** — SEO value, detailed reviews with photos. Declining but still relevant for some niches
- **Newsletter Feature** — growing format, highly engaged audience

### Creator's Choice
Many creators prefer to decide the format after visiting the restaurant, because the experience itself determines what content will work best. A photogenic dish might inspire a carousel; an energetic kitchen might call for a reel; a compelling chef story might become a longer video. Smart campaigns let creators choose.

## Campaign Types — From the Creator's Perspective

Instead of meaningless package names, campaigns should describe what the creator is actually doing. Here's how creators think about different types of work:

### Visit & Post
The simplest and most common format. Creator visits the restaurant, creates content from the experience, posts it.

- **What the creator does:** Visits on their own schedule, orders what interests them, films/photographs, creates and posts content
- **What the restaurant provides:** Complimentary meal + cash compensation
- **Deliverables:** Typically 1-2 posts (format at creator's discretion unless specified)
- **Timeline:** Usually 1-2 weeks from visit to post
- **Best for:** General awareness, introducing a restaurant to the creator's audience

### Menu Highlight
Creator features specific dishes, a new menu, or seasonal offerings. More directed than a general visit.

- **What the creator does:** Coordinates with restaurant on which items to feature, visits to try them, creates focused content around those items
- **What the restaurant provides:** Complimentary tasting of featured items + cash compensation
- **Deliverables:** 1-3 posts focused on the specific menu items
- **Timeline:** Tied to menu launch or seasonal window
- **Best for:** New menu launches, seasonal specials, signature dish awareness

### Event Coverage
Creator attends and covers a restaurant event — opening night, tasting event, chef's dinner, holiday special.

- **What the creator does:** Attends event, creates real-time content (stories) + polished post-event content (reels/posts)
- **What the restaurant provides:** Event access + complimentary F&B + cash compensation
- **Deliverables:** Real-time stories during event + 1-2 polished posts after
- **Timeline:** Content starts during event, polished posts within 1 week
- **Best for:** Openings, special events, generating buzz and FOMO

### Behind the Scenes
Creator gets kitchen/prep access to show the making of dishes, chef interviews, sourcing stories. This is premium content that audiences love.

- **What the creator does:** Spends extended time at restaurant (2-4 hours), films in kitchen, interviews chef/staff, creates story-driven content
- **What the restaurant provides:** Full access + chef/staff time + complimentary dining + higher cash compensation
- **Deliverables:** 1-2 long-form or short-form videos with narrative arc
- **Timeline:** 1-3 weeks for production and editing
- **Best for:** Establishing restaurant credibility, chef-driven brands, premium positioning

### Ongoing Partnership
Multi-visit deal over weeks or months. Creator becomes a regular ambassador for the restaurant. This is the most valuable type for both sides.

- **What the creator does:** Visits regularly (e.g., monthly), creates content consistently, builds genuine relationship with the restaurant
- **What the restaurant provides:** Standing comp + monthly retainer + possible revenue share on attributable traffic
- **Deliverables:** Agreed-upon cadence (e.g., 2 posts/month for 3 months)
- **Timeline:** 1-6 month commitment
- **Best for:** Building deep audience association between creator and restaurant

## What Makes a Creator Say Yes

When a creator evaluates a potential campaign on Spot, they're asking themselves:

1. **"Does this restaurant match my brand?"** — A luxury food creator won't promote a fast-casual chain. A street food TikToker won't film at a fine dining spot. Aesthetic, price point, cuisine, and vibe all need to align.

2. **"Will this content perform?"** — Is there something visually interesting, story-worthy, or trend-relevant? Beautiful plating, a unique concept, a compelling origin story, great ambiance?

3. **"Is the compensation fair?"** — For my audience size and engagement, is the pay reasonable? Is the restaurant asking for too much relative to what they're offering?

4. **"How much creative freedom do I have?"** — Can I tell the story my way? Or am I basically making an ad?

5. **"Is this professional?"** — Are the terms clear? Is the timeline reasonable? Will I actually get paid?

## What Makes a Creator Say No

1. **"Make us a viral video"** — Creators can't guarantee virality. Campaigns structured around guaranteed outcomes rather than quality content are red flags.

2. **"Post exactly this with these hashtags"** — Over-scripted campaigns feel fake and perform poorly. Audiences can spot an ad immediately.

3. **"We'll pay you in exposure"** — Free food alone isn't compensation for content that takes hours to produce. Even emerging creators know this (or should).

4. **"We need it by tomorrow"** — Rushed timelines produce bad content. Good food content needs planning, good lighting, and editing time.

5. **"Don't post about any other restaurants for 3 months"** — Unreasonable exclusivity kills the deal unless compensation reflects it.

## Applying This to Spot Platform Features

When building any creator-facing feature, ask:

- **Does this respect the creator's time?** — Minimize friction, pre-fill what you can, don't make them repeat information
- **Does this give them creative control?** — Describe scope and goals, not scripts
- **Does this feel professional?** — Clear terms, fair compensation guidance, reliable timelines
- **Does this help their content perform?** — Surface what's interesting about the restaurant, share visual assets, highlight what's trending
- **Does this make them money?** — Ultimately, Spot needs to be a place where creators find well-paying, brand-aligned partnerships efficiently
